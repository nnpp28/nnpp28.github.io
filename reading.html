Reading Reflections

Susan Sontag 'On Photography'

As Sontag noted, photography does more than documents reality. A photograph reflects choices of a person behind the camera and can shape how its subject is understood. A photograph can tell stories and narratives, influence reality, and even change history. Hence, the power a photograph holds should not be dismissed, but examined carefully.

A few years ago, I discovered a Belgian photographer who photographed people and everyday life in my home country. I was immediately drawn to pictures as they transformed ordinary scenes into something dramatic, colorful and almost surreal. Familiar people and places I grew up thinking of as dull and boring suddenly looked so bright and comic. I found this theatrical representation extremely inspiring and sort of genius. In a way, it helped me feel more connected to my home country while being so far away, while also being able to see people and places differently from how I was used to seeing them.

Furthermore, this particular style of photography was close to what I had always found interesting about photography myself: noticing something ordinary and giving it a new life through angle, color, framing or composition. I was so obsessed with the creativity and campness of the photographs that I completely forgot to consider the kind of representation these pictures were creating. Because I knew the culture and recognized the setting, I recognized the exaggeration as playful or artistic. But someone unfamiliar with the country might see the photographs differently. Instead of ordinary people represented creatively, they might seem as strange, grotesque, even freak-like.

This revelation led me to reconsider my own habit of photographing stranger or ordinary scenes simply because I found them visually interesting. I realized that choosing a certain angle, expression, gesture, or moment does not just document a person. It also assigns meaning to them. What is even more important is the kind of power I am exercising by telling stories about people they themselves never consented to, and giving them meanings they may never have intended.

Your Brain on ChatGPT

I first heard about ChatGPT probably two or three years ago, and I remember being surprised by how often and for what purposes my friends were using it. At the time, it seemed so new and kind of ridiculous to ask AI to plan something or do simple tasks that people had been doing by themselves not so long before. But once I tried it, I also became attached to the idea of always being able to get a fast answer, organize my thoughts, or think something through quickly.

Now, only a few years later, AI already feels deeply integrated into everyday life. It is not just an optional fun thing that can help you plan your day or a vacation. It is becoming an expectation at school, at work, and in many other areas of life. At first, it was introduced as a helpful tool to save time. Now it feels like it is being pushed from everywhere.

One of the main fears around AI is that it can make us think less. I do think AI can be useful if it is used consciously, as a tool that helps you rather than something that completely replaces your own work. But I also wonder how realistic that is for most people. Everyday life is already so stressful, exhausting, and focused on being productive that people are constantly looking for ways to make things easier. If AI can write, plan, explain, organize, or even make decisions faster, many people will probably use it as a shortcut.

I also think AI fits very well into capitalism and individualism. You can always get an answer without waiting for another person, and you can even get a kind of relationship or emotional response without putting much time or effort into building a real connection. Talking to real people requires patience, vulnerability, compromise, and care. Talking to AI only requires internet access. That is convenient, but I also find it very concerning.

If we slowly stop reaching out to each other, stop building friendships, stop asking other people for help, and stop being there for each other, then we become more isolated. And when people are isolated, exhausted, and dependent on technology, I think it becomes easier to influence how they think and what they accept as normal, good, or bad.

This is what worries me most. AI can definitely be helpful and can probably improve many areas of life, but I think for many people it risks becoming less of a tool and more of a replacement for effort, thought, and even human connection. Maybe we can learn to use it in a healthier way, but I am not sure whether our current way of living really encourages that.

Motherhood in Crisis

Each of the stories in Motherhood in Crisis is extremely painful and personal, and it is heartbreaking to see women struggle in different parts of the world. However, the last story, about Tetteh, was especially haunting to me, partly because of the way it was presented through illustrations rather than photographs. Unlike in some of the other stories, we don’t get to see a real picture of Tetteh, and instead have to imagine her through the portrayal created by the illustrator. I found this choice particularly interesting because of how it affected my perception of her story and the emotions I experienced while reading it.

For me, the illustrations created a certain distance from the painful reality of Tetteh’s experiences. Although I knew that her story was real, seeing it through illustrations somehow made it feel almost half-real. I think this has something to do with the nature of illustrations, as they allow for much more creative interpretation than photographs. There is always an understanding that what we see is someone’s representation of reality rather than reality itself. In a way, I think this allowed me to distance myself from the suffering of a real person, perhaps because it was simply too painful to fully imagine what she had gone through. At the same time, I wonder whether this was just my own way of protecting myself from having to confront such a devastating story.

I think seeing actual photographs of Tetteh, especially photographs showing the experiences described in the story, would have affected me differently. It would mean having to face a real person, in her body and flesh, and acknowledge that all this pain and suffering happened to her. However, despite creating this distance, the illustrations were still extremely emotional. In some ways, they even felt more intimate than photographs might have been. They allowed us to follow Tetteh’s experiences closely and imagine not only what was happening to her, but also what she might have been feeling. Unlike photography, illustrations can portray situations that were never documented and give a visual form to memories and emotions that might otherwise be difficult to communicate through text alone.

I also noticed a strong contrast between the illustrated story and the photographs and videos of activists discussing women’s incarceration. After following Tetteh’s experiences through illustrations, seeing real people talking about the issue almost felt detached from her story. While the illustrations made me feel emotionally connected to Tetteh, the photographs and videos seemed to serve a different purpose. They helped explain the broader problem and put her personal experience into a social and political context, but they did not affect me emotionally in the same way.

I think this contrast shows how different forms of visual storytelling can shape our perception of the same issue. Illustrations don’t necessarily make a story less real or less powerful, even if they create some distance from reality. In Tetteh’s case, I think they made it possible to communicate an extremely painful experience in a way that was both intimate and somewhat easier to confront.
